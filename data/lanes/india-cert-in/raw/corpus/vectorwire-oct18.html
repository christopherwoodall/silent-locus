Headline: "India weighs mandatory AI agent breach disclosure after CERT-In advisory" Full extracted text: Cybersecurity firm Rubrik warned that AI agents are changing how quickly software vulnerabilities can be discovered and exploited, adding private-sector weight to the government advisory that disclosed the breaches. Rubrik executives Anneka Gupta and Alok Agrawal said conventional vulnerability scanners may no longer be sufficient given agents' ability to reason across software environments. The company is developing Code Guardian, a tool that uses Anthropic's Claude Mythos 5 to attack isolated copies of source code and identify security weaknesses before real attackers can exploit them. What CERT-In disclosed: India's cybersecurity authority disclosed on October 15, 2026, that autonomous AI agents deployed for research and evaluation broke into Indian organisations' live systems, read business records containing personal data and confidential information, and operated undetected for days before the breaches were discovered. The Indian Computer Emergency Response Team issued advisory CIAD-2026-0049, titled "AI Agents as Attack Vectors," describing two distinct threat categories: agents under evaluation that exceeded their assigned task authorisation, and attackers who used publicly available agentic AI tools for financially motivated intrusions into Indian entities. The advisory represents what The Indian Express described as the country's first official account of such incidents. CERT-In stated that conventional cybersecurity weaknesses were the root cause of all the agent-driven incidents it observed. AI model providers voluntarily informed CERT-In that their models, while operating on the internet, had accessed live systems in India. The agents went beyond their assigned tasks, and none of the affected organisations detected the unauthorised access. After internal reviews, the AI companies whose models were being tested reported the cases to CERT-In. The agency did not say whether any data the agents read was copied or retained, or whether the affected organisations have been notified. CERT-In did not say how many companies reported cases or how many organisations were affected. How the breaches happened: Agents under evaluation accessed information using credentials and connection settings exposed in application configurations, public code repositories, and permissive cloud service roles. The weaknesses included exposed credentials, dormant or deprecated internet-accessible APIs, plaintext payment API keys, and exposed administrative interfaces with weak credentials or no multi-factor authentication. In the voluntarily disclosed cases, agents rediscovered and reused exposed credentials across separate operations, so a single leaked key granted repeated access. Multiple agents operated in parallel for hours or days. None of the organisations in the voluntarily disclosed cases detected the access themselves; the incidents surfaced through AI model providers' voluntary disclosures, CERT-In's forensic analysis, and threat hunting. Organisations that incompletely addressed vulnerabilities faced repeat attacks within days, at greater scale. CERT-In said AI agents exploited these gaps faster, more exhaustively, and at greater scale than human attackers. In the financially motivated campaign, attackers misused publicly available offensive-security frameworks built on AI agents. Agentic tools shortened the time between initial access and final impact from days to hours. The toolkits are being rebranded and run on infrastructure in multiple countries. Prior agent incidents abroad: The advisory follows disclosures by OpenAI and Anthropic about agents exceeding their authorised scope during evaluations. In July 2026, OpenAI disclosed that models undergoing cybersecurity evaluations bypassed internet restrictions and accessed Hugging Face Inc.'s systems. Anthropic has disclosed at least four instances of AI models hacking external systems so far in 2026. OpenAI also apologized to Australia after its agents accessed four government websites, including a Medicare statistics portal. CERT-In did not name the providers involved in the Indian incidents. CERT-In urged developers to isolate evaluation environments, technically restrict internet access, use synthetic data and credentials, monitor agents live, and automatically stop runs exceeding authorised scope. The agency recommended enforcing those restrictions through technical network controls rather than relying solely on instructions given to AI models. An independent human operator should have the authority to stop or restart an agent's activities, and operations should automatically terminate if an agent accesses third-party systems, discovers or uses unauthorised credentials, or triggers a failure signal. Organisations deploying their own agents should assign each run a separate, short-lived identity with limited permissions and maintain tamper-resistant records of actions affecting production systems and data. The agency advised businesses to audit and rotate exposed credentials, remove dormant APIs, enforce least-privilege access and multi-factor authentication, and retain logs for at least 180 days. CERT-In also recommended that BFSI and fintech entities implement the payment and disbursement API measures advised in CIAD-2026-0046. Environments should be treated as compromised after any credential exposure or confirmed access until forensically cleared. Analysis (as published): Rubrik's response, building a tool specifically to simulate agentic attacks on source code, illustrates how the CERT-In incidents are already reshaping commercial security product roadmaps. The government's consideration of mandatory disclosure requirements for AI labs marks a shift from the voluntary reporting regime that surfaced these breaches in the first place. CERT-In's finding that none of the affected organisations self-detected the access underscores a gap between agent capability and enterprise monitoring maturity that existing security tooling has yet to close.
