Gold Registry: start from components that are already fixed See how Safeguard would have helped Quick check Sources A report from VULONE, dated 18 October 2026, says one China-linked, Chinese-speaking operator used AI agents to run 66 intrusion projects across Asia between 24 September and 14 October, and that 48 reached some level of access. Targets included government bodies in Vietnam, Taiwan, Indonesia and India. Check for the published indicators, change default logins and retire old software now. 
 This post covers what the report says and what it does not, how the AI agents were used at a high level, every indicator VULONE published, an ATT&CK mapping, step by step detection and remediation commands for each platform involved, and what would have helped. It goes deeper than our earlier pieces on the same trend: CERT-In's advisory on AI agent attack vectors and what AI-found bugs like PHP2Shell and PwnEscape mean for defenders . 
 One point first, because headlines have blurred it. The figure of 48 is the operator's own tally, recorded by the operator's tooling. VULONE says it checked the strongest cases against exfiltration logs and listener captures where they survived, and that victim side verification is still pending. None of the named organisations had publicly confirmed a compromise in the sources we read. 
 What the report says 
 VULONE's Research Team published "China-Linked Hackers Targeted Governments Across Asia. We Hacked Their Infrastructure." The byline is dated 18 October 2026 and the blog listing shows 19 October. SecurityOnline covered it on 22 October under a headline describing 48 government and corporate targets. 
 The story starts with a tip from the researcher @TheM3gatr0n on X, according to VULONE. A Python web server on 202.61.139.15 was serving the host's /tmp directory on port 9000 with no authentication. VULONE says that server started at 22:13 UTC on 29 September 2026, and the same listing was mirrored on 202.61.139.137 . The directory held 339 files and, by VULONE's count, received more than 753,000 requests from 19,307 unique IP addresses while it was exposed. In other words, a lot of people besides the researchers saw it. 
 VULONE then went further than the open directory. It says it accessed the host and collected evidence under authorization, and it does not disclose how it got in, arguing there is no reason to publish the way in. It preserved logs before further interaction, hashed and timestamped its archives, and handled the material as if it might be used in court. It collected a targeted subset of 16.4 GB rather than a full disk image.

 Attribution, in VULONE's own terms 
 VULONE separates two judgements, and it is worth keeping them apart. 

 On the infrastructure: VULONE says, "We assess with high confidence" that the command and control infrastructure belonged to a single operator or a very small group. 
 On who that operator is: VULONE describes the operator as China-linked, Chinese-speaking and financially motivated. It says the artifacts it examined showed no sign of state sponsorship, while noting that state interest cannot be excluded from the outside. 

 The language clues, according to SecurityOnline's summary, include Simplified Chinese in script comments, task hints and commit messages, and partial funding through a Tencent-operated AI service. VULONE itself warns that language indicators can be manufactured. The operator's own rules required every payload and file written to a victim to be English or ASCII, with Chinese comments forbidden, so victim side evidence would not carry those clues anyway. 
 The operator's physical location is unknown. VULONE flags one anomaly: on 5 October a mobile connection in Kenya fetched scripts from the exposed directory.

 How the AI agents were used 
 This section stays at the level of architecture and operating choices. It does not describe how any target was exploited. 
 The platform: Cairn 
 VULONE says the operator built the platform on Cairn, an open source framework. Cairn's own README describes it as a general purpose AI state space search engine: give it a starting point and a goal, and its workers search for a path. Penetration testing is the first domain its authors validated. It uses a blackboard design with three shared primitives, which the README calls Facts (confirmed findings), Intents (planned explorations) and Hints (human guidance). Worker agents coordinate only through that board. A dispatcher schedules tasks and manages worker containers, and the supported LLM backends are Claude Code, Codex and Pi. The project is licensed AGPL-3.0 and its README says to use it only where you have explicit authorization. 
 VULONE is explicit that using a public tool does not identify its author, and says it found nothing connecting Cairn's maintainers to this operator. Cairn has a public container image and several hundred forks, per VULONE. 
 How this operator ran it 
 According to VULONE: 

 One dispatcher, many short lived workers. A dispatcher on the VPS scheduled up to 116 Kali Linux based worker containers using host networking, with dozens active at once. 
 One model behind a local gateway. Workers sent their reasoning through a single local LLM gateway to a model identified as global:deepseek-v4.1-flash . Two other identifiers appeared in provider traffic: cline-pass/deepseek-v4.1-flash and deepseek/deepseek-v4.1-flash . 
 State in one database. Project state lived in a single SQLite database, and completed work was archived to disk. 
 A farmed account pool. Six monthly LLM subscription accounts were registered together in late September; at least one was in arrears by $147.48 when collected. A scheduled task signed in 16 Gmail-based CodeBuddy identities every day to collect about 30 free credits each, roughly 480 a day. The operator held fallback credentials for at least two other LLM services. The account pool software was third party open source, adapted by the operator. 
 A human setting goals. VULONE describes the operator directing agents with objectives and constraints while the agents did most of the enumeration, exploitation and post-exploitation collection. It says plainly that this was not fully autonomous. 

 The operator's rules 
 VULONE quotes or paraphrases rules the operator gave the agents. Defenders should read them as a list of things the attacker was trying to hide from you. 

 A project only counts as complete at a webshell or system command execution. 
 Commands go through POST requests only, to reduce what shows in logs. 
 Clean up evidence directories. 
 Write everything placed on a victim in English or ASCII; no Chinese comments. 

 The POST-only rule is the most useful for defenders. Many web servers log the request line and status for POST requests but not the body, and many teams only alert on unusual GET parameters. An agent that sends every command in a POST body to a script file is quiet in those logs unless you watch for new script files and unusual POST targets. 
 Where the access came from 
 The striking part of the report is how old and ordinary most of the entry points were. VULONE lists, among others: 

 Shellshock (CVE-2014-6271) reached through a mail gateway on a RHEL5-era system, giving execution as a mail service user. 
 JBoss Seam expression language injection (CVE-2010-1871), a 2010 flaw that was still exploitable on one government site. 
 Default admin/admin credentials on a WSO2 API Manager console. 
 Anonymous FTP with write access, followed by SQL Server's xp_cmdshell . 
 ASP.NET ViewState deserialization leading to a webshell. 
 An ecommerce platform chain from file read to root, a PHP logging library object injection, and a Ruby job queue eval injection. 
 An unauthenticated role check bypass with arbitrary file write, PL/SQL injection, SSRF chains, and a V8 issue in a Lambda based card renderer. 
 Credential reuse on a shared multi-brand platform, where one credential exposed every brand. 
 A root reverse shell on a Redis pod, set up by cron. 

 VULONE adds that against well-patched targets the operator's own notes logged shell-oriented attempts as failures, and that the operator then fell back to credentials. Its line on the volume of attempts is worth remembering: the persistence reflects machine patience, not human persistence. 
 What this tells us 
 The agents did not need new vulnerabilities. They needed a list of old ones and the patience to try them everywhere, at the cost of free credits and a cheap model. The AI did the tedious middle of the job; the human chose targets and set rules. Gambit reported a similar pattern in the separate SOUL campaign, where, according to The Next Web, access, when achieved, "usually took less than a day." And the AI raised throughput, not discipline: an open directory with no password exposed the whole platform for over two weeks.

 Detection: step by step 
 Work through these in order. Each step says what to look for and how to check it. Replace example paths with your own. All of these are read-only. 
 1. Network logs: the two primary addresses 
 Search firewall, proxy, NetFlow and DNS logs for any traffic to or from 202.61.139.15 and 202.61.139.137 since 24 September 2026, and for the listener ports VULONE lists. 
 # Linux web or proxy server: search access logs for the two addresses
grep -E '202\.61\.139\.(15|137)' /var/log/nginx/*.log /var/log/httpd/*log* 2>/dev/null

# Zeek: outbound connections to the addresses or exfiltration ports
zcat /opt/zeek/logs/2026-*/conn.*.log.gz | \
 zeek-cut ts id.orig_h id.resp_h id.resp_p | \
 awk '$3 ~ /^202\.61\.139\.(15|137)$/ || $4 ~ /^(4445|4446|8896|8899|8997|9099|9201|9955)$/'

 On Windows, the firewall log is a quick first pass if logging is on. 
 Select-String -Path "$env:SystemRoot\System32\LogFiles\Firewall\pfirewall.log" `
 -Pattern '202\.61\.139\.(15|137)'

 2. Outbound PUT requests to an /ev/ path 
 VULONE says exfiltration used PUT /ev/ to high ports on the C2 host. If your egress proxy logs method and path, search for that pattern to any destination, not just the known addresses. 
 grep -E '"PUT [^ ]*/ev/' /var/log/squid/access.log* 2>/dev/null

 3. The TLS certificate 
 If you record TLS certificate details at the edge (Zeek x509.log , a proxy, or an NDR tool), search for the serial or the fingerprint. 
 zcat /opt/zeek/logs/2026-*/x509.*.log.gz | \
 grep -i '0CE6C93BC4B91DE00956D73B1D3DF1211B2409D3'

 4. The operator's SSH key on your hosts 
 This is the most direct host check. Run it on every Linux host and container base you can reach, including root. 
 sudo grep -rF 'AAAAC3NzaC1lZDI1NTE5AAAAIKo+sIL3HUlVhjn6PDSGak' \
 /root/.ssh /home/*/.ssh /etc/ssh 2>/dev/null
sudo grep -rF 'us@us-VMware-Virtual-Platform' /root/.ssh /home/*/.ssh 2>/dev/null

 5. User agents and client strings in web logs 
 grep -E 'pi \((win32|linux) |Cairn/1\.0|ccswitch' /var/log/nginx/access.log* /var/log/httpd/access_log* 2>/dev/null

 For IIS, the default W3C logs live under %SystemDrive%\inetpub\logs\LogFiles . 
 Get-ChildItem 'C:\inetpub\logs\LogFiles' -Recurse -Filter *.log |
 Select-String -Pattern 'Cairn/1\.0|ccswitch|pi\+\((win32|linux)'

 IIS writes spaces in the user agent field as + , which is why the pattern differs. 
 6. New script files in web roots 
 Because commands went through POST to webshells, the file itself is often the best evidence. List script files created or changed since the campaign began. 
 # Linux: PHP, JSP and similar files changed since 24 September 2026
sudo find /var/www /srv /opt -type f \
 \( -name '*.php' -o -name '*.jsp' -o -name '*.jspx' -o -name '*.aspx' \) \
 -newermt '2026-09-24' -printf '%TY-%Tm-%Td %TH:%TM %p\n' 2>/dev/null | sort

 # Windows/IIS: .aspx, .ashx and .asmx files written since 24 September 2026
Get-ChildItem 'C:\inetpub' -Recurse -Include *.aspx,*.ashx,*.asmx -ErrorAction SilentlyContinue |
 Where-Object { $_.LastWriteTime -gt (Get-Date '2026-09-24') } |
 Select-Object LastWriteTime, FullName | Sort-Object LastWriteTime

 Then look at POST requests to any file that appears in that list. A script that only ever receives POSTs from one or two addresses deserves attention. 
 7. Volume patterns 
 VULONE describes machine patience: very large numbers of attempts, sustained over days. Look for a single source, or a small set of cloud addresses, making thousands of requests across many distinct paths and returning mostly errors, then suddenly a run of successful POSTs to one file. That shape fits automated agents better than any single string does. 
 8. Do not attribute on language 
 VULONE's advice here is specific. The operator required English and ASCII on victims, so the absence of Chinese strings tells you nothing. Do not rule a host in or out on language. 
 Remediation: step by step for each platform 
 These steps follow the entry points VULONE lists. Each is a configuration or patch task, not a response to a specific victim. 
 Admin consoles with default credentials (WSO2 and others) 

 List every admin console reachable from the internet: API managers, application servers, file managers, database admin tools. 
 For WSO2 products, check the super admin account settings in your configuration (in recent API Manager releases, the [super_admin] block in repository/conf/deployment.toml ) and confirm the password is not a default. Follow WSO2's documentation for changing it on your version. 
 Put consoles behind VPN or an allowlist. A console that must be reachable needs MFA. 
 Rotate any API tokens and client secrets issued by that console. 

 # Find WSO2 config files and show the super admin block for review
sudo find / -name deployment.toml -path '*repository/conf*' 2>/dev/null | \
 xargs -I{} sh -c 'echo "== {}"; grep -A4 "^\[super_admin\]" "{}"'

 Bash and old mail gateways 

 Check the installed bash version on every Linux host and image, including appliances you rarely log in to. 
 RHEL5-era systems are long out of support. Plan to retire them; until then, put them behind a filtering relay and limit who can reach them. 

 bash --version | head -1
rpm -q bash 2>/dev/null || dpkg -l bash 2>/dev/null | tail -1
cat /etc/redhat-release /etc/os-release 2>/dev/null | head -3

 For containers, check the image rather than a running shell:

 Microsoft's documentation says xp_cmdshell is disabled by default on new installations, that new code should not use it, and that if it must be used it should be enabled only for the task that needs it.

 If it is enabled and you do not need it, disable it with sp_configure and RECONFIGURE as Microsoft's page shows, setting the value to 0. Then check which logins hold sysadmin , because those can turn it back on. 
 SELECT p.name FROM sys.server_role_members m
JOIN sys.server_principals r ON m.role_principal_id = r.principal_id
JOIN sys.server_principals p ON m.member_principal_id = p.principal_id
WHERE r.name = 'sysadmin';

 ASP.NET ViewState 
 VULONE lists ViewState deserialization as the route to a webshell on one site. Microsoft's Threat Intelligence post from February 2025 covers the most common cause: machine keys copied from public documentation or code. Microsoft says it found over 3,000 publicly disclosed keys. Its guidance, in short: 

 Do not copy machine keys from public sources. Generate new ones. 
 For a single server, remove a fixed machineKey element so ASP.NET generates keys; for a web farm, rotate on every server with the same new values. 
 Encrypt the machineKey and connectionStrings sections of web.config . 
 Upgrade to ASP.NET 4.8 so AMSI is available, and use the attack surface reduction rule that blocks webshell creation on servers. 
 If keys were exploited, Microsoft says rotation is not enough: investigate fully and consider rebuilding from offline media. 

 # Find web.config files that pin a machineKey
Get-ChildItem 'C:\inetpub' -Recurse -Filter web.config -ErrorAction SilentlyContinue |
 Select-String -Pattern '<machineKey' | Select-Object Path, LineNumber

 Response: if you find a match 

 Preserve before you clean. Copy logs, the suspicious file and a memory image if you can. Note times in UTC. 
 Isolate the host from the internet but keep it running if you need memory evidence. 
 Do not just delete the webshell. VULONE describes credential collection from configuration files. Assume every secret on that host is exposed: database passwords, API tokens, cloud keys, service accounts. 
 Rotate in the right order. Rotate at the source (the database, the cloud provider, the API platform), then redeploy, then revoke the old secret. 
 Look sideways. Check other hosts for the SSH key, the same file names and the same POST targets. The operator reused credentials across shared platforms. 
 Rebuild rather than repair where you cannot rule out persistence. Microsoft's ViewState guidance makes the same point for exploited keys. 
 Report. Contact your national CERT. In India that is CERT-In, which has its own advisory on AI agent attack vectors . VULONE says it delivered technical detail to affected organisations directly; if you think you are one of them and have not heard, your CERT is the right channel.

 What would have helped 
 Nothing in this report was a zero-day. Every route VULONE describes was an old flaw, a default, or a setting someone had turned on. That is good news, because all of it is fixable with ordinary work done consistently. 
 Practices any team can use 

 An inventory of what is actually running. Every internet-facing host, its operating system release, its runtimes, and the components inside each application. The RHEL5-era gateway and the JBoss Seam site are the kind of systems that fall off lists. 
 Retire or fence what you cannot patch. If a system is past end of life, move it behind VPN or a filtering relay, or switch it off. 
 No default credentials, ever. Make "default password changed" a deployment gate, not a post-launch task. MITRE's mitigation for T1078.001 says to change defaults before production, and CISA's 2023 Secure by Design alert asks manufacturers to stop shipping static default passwords at all. 
 Keep admin consoles off the internet. API managers, app server consoles and database tools belong behind VPN, with MFA. 
 Remove dangerous switches. xp_cmdshell off, anonymous FTP write off, Redis authenticated and internal, machine keys generated rather than copied. 
 Separate credentials per brand and per environment. One shared credential should never unlock an entire estate. 
 Log POST targets and watch for new script files. The operator chose POST-only commands to stay quiet. File integrity monitoring on web roots costs little. 
 Assume volume. Agents will try every old route on every host. Rate limits, WAF rules for known old payload shapes, and alerting on bursts of errors followed by a success all help. 
 Govern your own agents. If you run AI agents internally, give each run its own short-lived credentials, limit egress, log every action, and require a human to approve changes to production. The architecture in this report is the same one legitimate tools use.

 Common mistakes 

 Reading 48 as 48 confirmed breaches. It is the operator's tally, partly corroborated. Check your own systems; do not wait for a confirmation that may never be public. 
 Searching only for the two C2 addresses. The operator used cloud and mobile addresses too, and the next operator will use different ones. Behaviour and file checks last longer. 
 Treating a clean GET log as a clean server. Commands came by POST. Look at files and POST targets. 
 Deleting the webshell and moving on. Credentials were collected. Rotate them. 
 Assuming AI means new bugs. Here it meant old bugs, tried patiently everywhere. 
 Running bash --version on the host and forgetting the images. Check container images and appliances too. 

 FAQ 
 Who published the report on AI agents breaching 48 targets in Asia? 
 VULONE's Research Team, in a report bylined 18 October 2026 and listed on its blog on 19 October. SecurityOnline and others covered it from 22 October. 
 Did the operator really breach 48 government agencies? 
 No. VULONE says 48 of 66 projects reached session level access in the operator's own records, and the 48 include both government and corporate targets. Six reached root or SYSTEM. Victim side verification is pending, and none of the named organisations had confirmed in the sources we read. 
 Is this a Chinese state operation? 
 VULONE describes a China-linked, Chinese-speaking, financially motivated operator, likely one person or a very small group. It found no evidence of state sponsorship but says state interest cannot be excluded from the outside. 
 Which AI models and tools were involved? 
 VULONE says the platform was built on the open source Cairn framework, with workers routed through a local gateway to a DeepSeek model identified as global:deepseek-v4.1-flash . The operator used six paid subscriptions and 16 CodeBuddy accounts farmed for free daily credits. 
 What are the most useful indicators to check first? 
 The two addresses 202.61.139.15 and 202.61.139.137 , the operator's SSH public key in authorized_keys files, the TLS certificate serial, and the pi (...) and Cairn/1.0 client strings in web logs. All are in the Technical details section. 
 Is this the same campaign as Gambit Security's SOUL report? 
 VULONE says the tooling ecosystem is the same with high confidence, but there are no shared indicators, so "same campaign" is not supported and "same operator" is possible but unproven. 
 Did VULONE notify the victims? 
 VULONE says technical details were delivered to affected organisations directly and thanks responders who engaged. It gives no dates or recipients, and it does not say whether national CERTs were notified. 
 Would Safeguard have stopped this? 
 No. Safeguard can show where old components such as outdated bash or Seam jars sit in your repositories and images, and help you fix them with human approval. It does not detect intrusions, check consoles for default passwords, or scan hosts it cannot see. 
 Fix it without waiting for upstream 
 In incidents like this one, most of the risk sits in two gaps. The first is the time between a fix being published and that fix actually running in your production builds. The second, and worse, is the time before any fix exists at all, while a maintainer works out a patch and a release schedule. Waiting through both gaps is a habit, not a requirement.
